## Full-text Pass 2 review (2026-09-25)

- `verification_status=full_text_verified`; earlier abstract-only statements are superseded. All 16 PDF pages read; article pp.1–12, Tables 1–4, Figs.4–6 and reference list checked. Article p.12 reference 1 explicitly cites the project's Volume II technical report `DOT-HS-805-079`, and says its own results are a partial presentation of that larger study. This is a distinct SAE analysis/reuse of underlying test data, not a duplicate of either DOT volume; the full DOT report is not locally available for point-by-point comparison.
- Five instrumented vehicles: Honda Accord, Chevrolet station wagon, Ford Econoline, VW Microbus, pickup/camper. Ottawa full-scale steady-yaw tunnel measured six aero coefficients; tire/vehicle parameters came from separate facilities. On-road STI/NHTSA eight-module fan facility offered nominal 35 mph over 120 ft. This paper reports a straight-pulse test at 50 mph in steering-fixed, steering-free, and active-driver closed-loop modes (pp.1–2). Nominal 35 mph crosswind at 50 mph gives about 35° relative yaw and 61 mph resultant airspeed (p.4).
- Table 1 p.4 presents *predicted steady-state* yaw rates, including VW Microbus 6.01°/s fixed and 5.50°/s free; Table 3 p.7 gives *measured average peak* Microbus 4.71±0.41°/s fixed and 5.06±0.56°/s free across seven runs each. These are different statistics, not a direct matched transient prediction. The VW has the largest observed open-loop peak yaw and lane departure among the five; its closed-loop driver workload is largest in Table 4, but steering correction reduces lane offset to about 0.9±0.5 ft (p.7).
- Fig.4 and article p.6 report little initial yaw-rate overshoot for this approximately 2 s, 120 ft straight pulse. Authors estimate a 60 ft disturbance is enough to reach a steady-like directional response at 50 mph and peaks occur within roughly 30 ft (p.12 conclusion). This is **conditional on this facility, vehicles, speeds and yaw output**; it does not refute shorter/steeper aerodynamic load overshoot experiments.
- Simplified open-loop yaw relation combines wind side force, the distance between aerodynamic and tire-force centers, tire restoring moment/understeer and steer-to-yaw gain (pp.2–5). Four of five calculated fixed-steer values lie below measured values; authors hypothesize steering free play, while VW prediction lies above, hypothesized due vehicle heading changing incident wind. Steering-free comparison fits four of five better (pp.7–8, Fig.6). Their stated accuracy is within about 50%, not a validated transient load model (p.12).
- Fig.5/Table 4 use a small driver-rating sample; the suggested 3°/s single-sided yaw-rate threshold for more-than-moderate control demand is an exploratory correlation from this cohort, not a universal safety limit. Extrapolations above the 35 mph fan maximum (Figs.8–16) are model predictions; no full-scale high-wind validation is supplied.
